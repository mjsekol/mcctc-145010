# Stakeholder Communication Guide
## 145010 Senior Capstone · Weeks 7-18, whenever you write to your stakeholder

**Use it:** before every message that is not routine. Your instructor reads your first contact
message before it is sent, and is copied on every message after that.

**Competencies this evidences:** 1.2.5 (communicate information for an intended audience and
purpose), 1.2.11 (write professional correspondence), 1.2.13 (identify stakeholders and solicit
their opinions), 2.13.2 (communicate rollout plans to key stakeholders in a timely manner), 1.4.8
(electronic media and network etiquette), 1.10.3 (communicate features, benefits, and
warranties).

---

## Why this exists

**Most students have never written to a client.** You have written to teachers, who are paid to
read what you send, and to friends, who know what you meant. Your stakeholder is neither. They are
a busy adult doing you a favor, and every message either makes that favor lighter or heavier.

**What the stakeholder needs to hear is usually not what you want to say.** You want to say how hard
you worked. They want to know what changed for them, what is at risk, and what they need to do.

**This is a skill, and it is graded.** Your communication record is part of Process & Documentation,
and your stakeholder's trust is most of Stakeholder Outcome.

---

## The rules for every message

1. **From your school account, with your instructor copied.** Never a personal account, personal
   phone, or social media. If a stakeholder texts or messages you personally, reply once through
   school email and tell your instructor.
2. **A subject line that says what the message is.** "Shift sign-up: week 12 update, at risk," not
   "hi" or "update."
3. **The point in the first two sentences.** Put anything they must act on at the top.
4. **One message, one purpose.** A question buried under an update does not get answered.
5. **Their words, not yours.** "The coordinator page," not "the admin CRUD view."
6. **Short sentences. No slang, no emoji, no text abbreviations.**
7. **Read it aloud before you send it.** If you would not say it to their face in a meeting, rewrite
   it.
8. **Never send when you are frustrated.** Save it as a draft and read it in the morning.
9. **No personal data about anyone** in any message. No attachments with real records.
10. **Commit what you sent** and add a line to your contact log.

---

## What professional looks like, next to what a student instinctively writes

| Instinct | Professional |
|---|---|
| "hey so i was wondering if maybe u could look at something" | "Could you review the attached requirements by Thursday? I have one specific question below." |
| "Sorry to bother you again, sorry, I know you are busy" | "Thank you for your time on this." Once. |
| "Everything is going great" | "Status: on track. You can now create next week's shifts yourself." |
| "It is kind of broken right now but I am working on it" | "The sign-up page is not saving claims. I found the cause and expect a fix by Wednesday." |
| "Let me know what you think" | "Is anything in these five criteria not what you meant? A reply by Friday lets me start building on Monday." |
| "I will have it done by Friday for sure" | "I expect to have it ready by Friday. I will tell you on Wednesday if that changes." |

## 1. First contact

**What they need to hear:** who you are, why them, what you are asking, how much of their time, and
that the school supervises it. In that order, in under 200 words.

**The draft** is in the [Stakeholder Finding worksheet](MCCTC_145010_Template_StakeholderFinding.md#the-contact-script).
Adapt it. Do not copy it.

**Three things not to do:**
1. Pitch an app before you understand their problem.
2. Promise anything: a feature, a date, support after graduation.
3. Follow up more than once in three school days.

---

## 2. Weekly update

**What they need to hear:** the status in one word, what changed for them, any problem and what
you are doing about it, and what you need from them with a date.

**The draft** is the [Weekly Stakeholder Update template](MCCTC_145010_Template_WeeklyStakeholderUpdate.md),
with a before-and-after example.

**Three things not to do:**
1. Describe your activity instead of their progress. "Worked on the database" says nothing to them.
2. Skip a week because there is nothing to report. There is always a status.
3. Save problems for next week, when you hope they will be fixed.

---

## 3. Bad news: we are behind

**What they need to hear:** what is late, what it affects for them, what you are doing, and when
they will know more. **Early.** Bad news loses value every day you hold it.

**How to deliver it without hiding it or over-apologizing:**
- **State it plainly in the first sentence.** Not in the third paragraph.
- **Apologize once, briefly, if it was your fault.** Then move to the plan. Repeated apology makes
  the reader manage your feelings instead of the problem.
- **Say what you are doing, not how bad you feel.**
- **Offer a choice when there is one.** Stakeholders like deciding more than being told.
- **Give a date for the next update.** Then keep it.

**A draft to adapt:**

```
Subject: <project>: the coordinator page will be late

Hello <title and last name>,

The page where you create shifts will not be ready this Friday as planned.
I expect it next Wednesday.

The reason is that the hosted database stopped accepting connections on
Tuesday, and fixing it took two days. The cause is found and the fix is in
place.

This means you would set up next week's shifts the usual way one more time.
If that is a problem, I can instead finish a simpler version by Friday that
creates shifts but does not yet let you edit them. Which would you prefer?

I will send you an update on Monday either way.

Thank you,
<first name>
```

**Three things not to do:**
1. Bury it: "Things are mostly on track, though there have been a few small delays."
2. Over-apologize: "I am so sorry, I feel terrible, I really let you down."
3. Blame: "The hosting company broke it." Even when true, it sounds like an excuse. Say what
   happened and what you did.

## 4. Scope change request

**Two situations, and they are handled differently.**

**You need to cut something.** What they need to hear: what is cut, why, what they still get, and
that you need their agreement in writing. Frame it around what protects the thing they care about
most.

```
Subject: <project>: request to change what is delivered

Hello <title and last name>,

I would like your agreement to one change to what I deliver.

I propose moving the printable weekly view to the list of future
improvements. Finishing it would put the coordinator page, which you told me
matters most, at risk of not being tested properly before Week 16.

Everything else we agreed stays the same. Acceptance criterion 6, the
printable view, would be removed from the agreement.

Could you reply to say whether you agree? I will not make the change
without your answer.

Thank you,
<first name>
```

**They ask for something new after Week 12.** What they need to hear: that the idea is heard and
recorded, that it will not be built now, and why that protects them.

```
That is a useful idea, and I have added it to the list of future
improvements that I will hand over with the project. The scope was locked in
Week 12 so that everything we agreed is finished and properly tested before
you rely on it. If you would like, I can include a short note on how someone
could add it later.
```

Record it in the [Change Request template](MCCTC_145010_Template_ChangeRequest.md) either way.

**Three things not to do:**
1. Say yes in the moment to be agreeable. That is how Week 16 becomes an argument.
2. Cut something quietly and hope nobody notices.
3. Say "my teacher will not let me." The scope lock protects them. Say that.

## 5. Requesting feedback

**What they need to hear:** exactly what to look at, one to three specific questions, how long it
will take, and the date you need it by, with the reason.

```
Subject: <project>: three questions on the requirements, reply by Thursday

Hello <title and last name>,

The attached requirements describe what the project will do. It should take
about ten minutes to read. I have three questions:

1. Requirement 3 says a volunteer can release their own shift until 24
   hours before it starts. Is 24 hours right?
2. Is anything you need missing from the list?
3. Is there anything here you did not expect to see?

A reply by Thursday lets me finish the design on Friday.

Thank you,
<first name>
```

**Three things not to do:**
1. Ask "any thoughts?" You will get none, or everything.
2. Send twelve documents at once.
3. Ask for feedback on something they cannot judge, like a code structure. Ask about what they
   will see and do.

## 6. Acceptance request

**What they need to hear:** the date and format of the acceptance run, how long it takes, what they
will do, that some tests are meant to show error messages, and that they decide in writing
afterward.

```
Subject: <project>: booking the acceptance check in Week 16

Hello <title and last name>,

The project is ready for the acceptance check we agreed in Week 8. It
takes about forty minutes. We go through each of the criteria you signed,
together, on the live system, and you decide afterward whether to accept
it.

Some of the checks test what happens when something goes wrong, so an
error message on screen is sometimes the right result. If anything fails
that we did not expect, I will record it and fix it afterward.

Could you meet at school on Wednesday or Thursday of next week, with my
instructor present? If neither works, I can send you the checks and
results in writing instead.

Thank you,
<first name>
```

**Three things not to do:**
1. Ask them to "sign off" before they have seen it work.
2. Change a criterion so it will pass.
3. Leave booking the run until the week it happens.

## 7. Handoff and training

**What they need to hear:** what they are receiving, when, who supports it after you leave, what
you promise and until when, and what to do when something goes wrong. **Send the rollout plan
before the handoff**, which is what 2.13.2 means by timely.

```
Subject: <project>: handoff plan, please review by Wednesday

Hello <title and last name>,

Attached is the plan for handing the project over to you. It covers:

- the training session, proposed for Thursday at school
- who at your organization looks after it once I have graduated
- what to do if it stops working, including how to go back to the old way
- what I will fix, and until when

Could you read it and tell me by Wednesday if anything should change,
especially the support section?

On Friday we complete the handoff checklist together, and you give your
final approval if you are satisfied.

Thank you,
<first name>
```

**Your warranty, in plain words.** Say exactly what you will fix and until when, usually the end of
Week 18, and that nothing after that is promised unless agreed in writing through the school.

**Three things not to do:**
1. Promise to "always be available." You will not be, and it breaks the school-channel rule.
2. Hand over without training and hope the user guide is enough.
3. Keep access, data, or passwords you no longer need.

## 8. When your stakeholder goes quiet

**It is common and usually not personal.** People get busy. Your job is to make replying quick and
to keep a record.

**Step 1 · the follow-up**, after one message with no reply in three school days. Short, with the
original quoted underneath and one simple question.

```
Subject: Re: <original subject>

Hello <title and last name>,

I wanted to follow up on the message below. The one thing I need is
<the question>, and a one-line reply is plenty. If this week is busy, could
you tell me a better time to check back?

Thank you,
<first name>
```

**Step 2 · tell your instructor**, after two contacts over five school days with no reply. At the
next standup, not at the end of the week. Your instructor contacts the stakeholder through the
school.

**Step 3 · the backup contact**, named in your acceptance agreement, if there is still no answer.
Your instructor is copied.

```
Subject: <project>: reaching <stakeholder role>

Hello <title and last name>,

I am the student building <project> with <stakeholder role>, who named you
as the backup contact. I have not been able to reach them for <n> school
days. I need <the specific thing> by <date> to stay on schedule. Would you be
able to help, or tell me who could?

Thank you,
<first name>
```

**Step 4 · keep building.** A quiet stakeholder does not stop your sprint. Record every step in your
contact log on the day it happens. **You are not scored down for a silence your record documents.**
See the [Control phase guide](MCCTC_145010_PhaseGuide_Control.md#if-your-stakeholder-disappears)
for what happens if nobody answers before acceptance.

**Three things not to do:**
1. Send daily reminders.
2. Contact them through a personal channel to get a faster answer.
3. Stop sending weekly updates because nobody replies. The updates are your record.
