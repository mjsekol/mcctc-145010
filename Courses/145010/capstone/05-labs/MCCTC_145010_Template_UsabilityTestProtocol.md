**Use with:** the [Usability Observation Sheet](MCCTC_145010_Template_UsabilityObservationSheet.md),

(determine satisfaction with measurement tools), 1.5.5 (how bias influences who a product serves).

---

## Why this exists

**You are not your user.** You know where everything is because you put it there. The only way to
find out what a stranger does not know is to watch one try, say nothing, and write it down.

**What a person did and what a person said are different evidence, and they disagree
constantly.** Someone who hunted for forty seconds and then said "that was fine" has given you two
facts. Only one of them is about your project.

**The hardest discipline is silence.** You will want to help within twenty seconds. The moment you
help, you have lost the measurement, because what you needed to know was whether they would have
found it.

---

## The rules

**These are conditions, not advice. Breaking one stops the session.**

1. **They say yes out loud** after you read the consent script as written.
2. **No recording of any kind.** No video, no audio, no screen capture, no photographs.
3. **No personal data leaves the session.** Participants are P1 to P5.
4. **No classmates in the formal five.** Classmates are for the pilot, which is labelled as a pilot.
5. **Nothing from a session goes into an AI tool.**
6. **Never alone with an adult you do not know.** At school, on an approved call, or on a visit the
   school arranged.
7. **You thank every participant in writing** before the last day of the course.

## Part 1 · Recruiting

**Start in Week 14.** A student who starts on Monday of Week 15 does not find five people.

**Who to ask, best first:**
1. **The real users**, reached through your stakeholder and your instructor.
2. **Adults in this building outside the program.** Your instructor keeps a list of people who
   have agreed.
3. **Students in other programs** who have never seen your project.
4. **Family at home**, with every rule above. A younger sibling only if they are twelve or older
   and a parent is in the room.

**What to say:**

> I am testing a project I built for a real client, and I need to watch a few people try it. It
> takes about fifteen minutes. I am testing the project, not you, and nothing is recorded. Would
> you be willing to help, and is there a time this week that works?

**Think about who is missing.** Five people who are all the same age, the same comfort with
technology, and the same first language will find the problems that kind of person has. Write down
who your five do not represent. It goes in your findings.

```markdown
## Recruiting
| P | Kind of participant (no names) | Why they fit | Session: Week 15, day and time | Where |

| P1 | <real user / building adult / other program / family> | | | |

| P3 | | | | |
| P4 | | | | |
| P5 | | | | |
| Backup | | | | |

**Who these five do not represent:** <...>
```

## Part 2 · The consent script, word for word

**Read it as written.** The sentences people drop when they paraphrase are the recording one and
the stopping one, and those are the two that matter.

> I am testing a project I built, and I am not testing you. Anything that goes wrong is information
> I need. I am going to ask you to do a few things and then stay quiet while you do them, and that
> is on purpose, not rude. I am not recording anything and I am not writing down your name. You can
> stop at any point, for any reason, and you do not have to tell me why. Is that okay?

**Wait for a yes out loud. A nod is not a yes.** If they hesitate, the answer is no. Thank them and
stop.

Then one more sentence:

> If you get stuck, stay stuck for a bit. That is the part I most need to see.

## Part 3 · The tasks

**Four to six tasks, written as goals, not instructions.** A goal says what the person wants. An
instruction says how to get it, which is the thing you are testing.

| Instruction (do not write these) | Goal (write these) |
|---|---|
| Click the Hours link. | Find out when the shop closes on Saturday. |
| Press Claim on the 6 pm shift. | You can help at the stand Friday evening. Sign yourself up. |
| Open the Alarms tab and acknowledge the alarm. | The panel says something is wrong. Deal with it the way you would on a real shift. |
| Type your question in the search box. | A customer wants to know how often to change the filter on their mower. Find the answer. |

**Each task has:** a starting state you set up before they arrive, a goal in their words, a reason
a real person would do it, and what counts as completed.

**At least one task covers accessibility or navigation:** reaching something several steps deep,
using the product on a phone, or finishing a task with the keyboard alone.

```markdown
## Tasks
### Task 1
Starting state: <what is on screen and what data exists before they start>
Read aloud:     "<the goal, in plain words, with a reason>"
Completed when: <the observable result>
Expected time:  <your guess, so you can compare>
Tests requirement: R<n>

### Task 2
...
```

**Pilot the tasks with a classmate on Monday.** A pilot always finds at least one of these: a task
the project cannot do, a task with the answer in its wording, or a task that takes far longer than
you expected. Fix them before a real participant sees them. Record what the pilot found.

## Part 4 · What you say, and what you do not

**The four things you may not say:**

```
1  the name of a control      "click Save", "there is a menu at the top"
2  "you can"                  "you can use the search box"
3  "did that work?"           tells them it was supposed to
4  the answer                 in any form, including your face
```

**The four things you may say:**

```
nothing                                   the default, and usually right
"What are you trying to do right now?"
"What did you expect to happen?"
"Take your time."
```

**If they ask "am I doing this right?"** say: "There is no right way. I want to see what you would
actually do." Not yes, not no, and not "you are doing fine," which is yes with a friendly voice.

**If they are stuck for three minutes**, say "Let us move to the next one," and write down where
they were stuck. That is a finding, not a failed session.

**Stop at fifteen minutes**, even mid-task. Stopping on time is how you get a yes next time.

---

## Part 5 · Debrief questions, after the tasks, never during

Ask two or three. Write the answers word for word and label them as opinions.

1. If you had to do that again tomorrow, what would you do differently?
2. Was there a moment you were not sure what would happen next?
3. What would you call <the main thing in the product>? *(Their word may be better than yours.)*
4. Is there anything you expected to find and did not?

**Not:** "Did you like it?" "Was it clear?" "Any suggestions?" Those questions invite politeness and
return nothing you can act on.

Then:

> That is everything. Thank you. This was genuinely useful.

---

## Part 6 · The trap, and the line for that moment

**The trap:** a participant struggles, or says something critical, and you start explaining why the
design is the way it is. The moment you defend the design, you have stopped learning from the
session.

**The line to say to yourself, and what your instructor will say if they see it happen:**

> **What they did is the data. Your reasons are not. Write it down and stay quiet.**

**And the line to say to the participant, if you have already started explaining:**

> Sorry, ignore me. Please carry on the way you were.

---

## Part 7 · From sessions to a prioritized change list

**Write up each session the same day**, from your observation sheet, into
`sessions/P<n>.md`. Number the lines so findings can cite them as `P3 L7`.

**Then build the findings table.** One row per problem, not per participant.

```markdown
## Findings
| # | What happened | How many of the 5 | Evidence | Severity | Decision |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| F1 | <what they did, not what they said> | 3 of 5 | P1 L4, P2 L9, P5 L3 | <see below> | <change / no change, and why> |
```

**Severity:**
- **Critical:** the person could not complete a task a requirement depends on.
- **Serious:** they completed it with a wrong attempt or a long delay.
- **Minor:** hesitation or a comment, no effect on completion.

**Prioritize by severity first, then by count.** A critical problem seen once outranks a minor one
seen five times.

**Counts, never percentages.** "3 of 5 people I watched" is exactly what you know. "60 percent of
users" claims a survey you did not do.

**Every change gets four lines** in your change list:

```
Finding:      F1, 3 of 5 chose the wrong week
Change:       the current week is selected when the page opens
Prediction:   a participant reaches the right week with no wrong attempts
How I know:   re-test Task 2 with at least one new person after the change
```

**A change with no finding above it is a preference.** Preferences are allowed. Write them in a
separate list and call them preferences.

**Finally, the limits:** who your five did not represent, which sessions had a slip (you helped, or
asked "did that work?"), and what five sessions cannot tell you.
