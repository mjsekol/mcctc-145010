## Why this exists

**A capstone without a real person drifts.** You make every decision by guessing what a user
would want, and every guess is the one that is most fun to build. A real person says "that is
not what I need" in Week 8, which is the cheapest week in the capstone to hear it.

Finding that person is not luck. It is a search, and you can do most of it in one period.

**The hardest part is sending the first message.** Almost everyone you ask will be kinder about
it than you expect. The most common reason a student has no stakeholder on Friday is that the
message was never sent.

---

## The rules before you contact anyone

1. **Your instructor approves your candidate before you write to them.**
2. **Your instructor reads your first message before you send it.**
3. **You write from your school email account, with your instructor copied.** Never a personal
   account, a personal phone number, or social media.
4. **You never meet a stakeholder alone.** Meetings are at school, on a call your instructor
   approved, or on a visit the school arranged.
5. **Your parent or guardian knows who your stakeholder is.**
6. **A parent or guardian is not your stakeholder** unless your instructor decides otherwise. A
   stakeholder has to be someone who will tell you honestly that something does not work.

---

## What counts as a stakeholder

A stakeholder for this capstone is **an adult outside this classroom who has the problem, or is
responsible for it, and who can say whether your work solved it.**

| Counts | Does not count |
|---|---|
| The person at a workplace who deals with the problem every week | "Small business owners" in general |
| An adult in this building outside this program who has a real, recurring problem | A classmate, or this course's instructor |
| A coach, advisor, or volunteer coordinator responsible for the problem | Someone who "would probably use it" |
| A manager at your part-time job | A company you have no way to reach |

**A stakeholder has authority over the problem**, or can get it from someone who does. If they
cannot say yes to your acceptance criteria, find out who can.

## Part 2 · Eight places to look, ranked

These are ranked by how likely a student is to get a yes. Work down the list. For each place,
write one real candidate if you have one, or "none" if you do not.

| Rank | Where to look | Why it often says yes | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **An adult in this building outside this program**: another program's instructor, the front office, the library or media center, maintenance, food service, the counselors' office | They are here, school channels already connect you, and they have recurring problems nobody has time to fix | Choose a problem they own, not one they have heard about |
| 2 | **Your part-time job**: a shift lead or manager | They know you, and workplaces are full of repeated paperwork | Their manager may need to approve it. Ask early |
| 3 | **A club, team, or activity you belong to**: a coach, advisor, or organizer | They already rely on you, and their problems are concrete and weekly | Pick a problem the adult owns, not one the players have |
| 4 | **Your home high school**: a teacher or staff member you know | Schools have endless small, real problems | No student data may be involved, ever |
| 5 | **A community organization you or your family volunteer with**: a food pantry, a youth league, a place of worship, a shelter | Volunteer-run groups rarely have anyone to build software | Personal data about the people they serve is off limits |
| 6 | **A family member's workplace**, where the stakeholder is the person there who owns the problem | Someone can introduce you | The stakeholder must be someone who will be honest with you, not your relative |
| 7 | **A small local business you are a regular customer of** | Owners notice repeat customers | A cold request. Expect several no answers before a yes |
| 8 | **The program's employer partners and advisory committee, through your instructor only** | They support this program | Capacity is limited, and your instructor makes the contact |

## The contact script

**What the person needs to know in the first message**, in this order: who you are, why them,
what you are asking for, how much of their time it takes, and that it is supervised by the school.

**What they do not need:** your whole idea, technical words, or an apology for asking.

### By email, from your school account, instructor copied

Adapt this. Do not copy it word for word. It should sound like you on your most professional day.

```
Subject: Request: a senior capstone project at the career center

Dear <title and last name>,

My name is <first name>, and I am a senior in the AI Automation and Software
Development program at the career center. My instructor, <instructor name>,
is copied on this message.

For my senior capstone I build a working piece of software for a real person
with a real problem. I am writing to you because <one specific sentence about
the problem you believe they have, and how you know about it>.

I would like to ask whether you would be willing to be my project's
stakeholder. That would mean a short conversation this week or next about how
the problem works today, reviewing a written proposal, and about fifteen
minutes of contact most weeks until the end of the school year. There is no
cost to you, and the project is supervised by my instructor.

Would you be open to a short conversation? I can work around your schedule.

Thank you for considering it.

<first and last name>
Senior, AI Automation and Software Development
<school name>
```

### In person, at school

Keep it short and make it comfortable for them to say no.

> Hi, I am <name>, a senior in the software program. I am looking for someone with a real
> problem I could build a solution for as my senior capstone. You mentioned <the problem>, and I
> wondered if you would be willing to talk with me about it for fifteen minutes this week. My
> instructor supervises the project. It is completely fine to say no.

**Then follow up the same day in writing**, from your school account, with your instructor
copied. A spoken yes is not recorded until it is written.

### What not to do

1. **Do not pitch a finished idea.** "I want to build you an app" makes them evaluate an app. "I
   want to understand the problem" makes them talk about the problem, which is what you need.
2. **Do not promise anything.** Not a delivery date, not a feature, not support after graduation.
   The proposal is where promises are made, in writing, after the scope check.
3. **Do not follow up more than once in three school days.** One polite reminder, then tell your
   instructor.

## When they say no

Thank them in one line and mean it. Then go to your backup, the same day. **A no in Week 7 costs
an afternoon. A no in Week 10 costs the project.**
