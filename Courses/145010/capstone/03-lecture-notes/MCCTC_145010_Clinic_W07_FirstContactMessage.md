# Clinic · The First Message to a Stranger
## 145010 Senior Capstone · Week 7, Tuesday · 15 minutes · Define

**Slides for this clinic:** This clinic has no slide outline. It runs at the board.

**When this clinic runs.** Week 7, Tuesday, or any week the room shows this signal: drafts that
pitch an app, apologize three times, or have no subject line.

**If you missed it,** you can learn the skill from this file alone.

**Competencies:** 1.2.11 (write professional correspondence), 1.4.8 (electronic media and network

---

## Why this exists

**The person you are writing to owes you nothing.** They will read your message between two other
things they have to do. If they cannot tell in a few seconds who you are and what you want, they
will set it aside, and "later" often becomes never.

**No reply means no stakeholder by Friday.** The stakeholder is a fixed milestone for Week 7,
Friday. The most common reason a student misses it is not a no. It is a message that was never
sent, or one that was sent and could not be answered.

Three drafts show up in this room every year. The pitch, which describes an app the person never
asked for. The apology spiral, which says sorry until the request disappears. And the blank
subject line, which looks like spam. Each one costs you a reply. This clinic fixes all three.

---

## The skill in plain language

A first contact message answers five questions, **in this order**, in under 200 words:

1. **Who are you?** First name, senior, the program, the school. Your instructor is copied.
2. **Why them?** One specific sentence about the problem you believe they have, and how you know.
3. **What are you asking for?** A conversation about the problem. Not an app. Not a yes to a project.
4. **How much of their time?** Stated honestly, including what comes later if it goes well.
5. **Who supervises it?** Your instructor, through the school.

Around those five, three pieces of structure:

- **A subject line that says what the message is.** "Request: a short conversation about building
  repair requests" can be answered. "Hi" cannot.
- **A clear way to say no.** Write "It is completely fine to say no." People say yes more readily
  when no is allowed.
- **One thank-you.** Once, at the end.

**The rules do not bend.** Your instructor approves your candidate first. Your instructor reads the
message before it is sent. It goes from your school account with your instructor copied. Never a
personal account, a personal phone number, or social media. The full list is in the
[Stakeholder Finding worksheet](../05-labs/MCCTC_145010_Template_StakeholderFinding.md#the-rules-before-you-contact-anyone).

## Worked example 1 · The pitch, rewritten

*Composite, not a real organization or person.* A senior noticed repair requests stuck on sticky
notes on the door of the career center's maintenance office. The candidate is the maintenance
supervisor.

**Before:**

```
Subject:

Hi,

I'm a senior in the software program and I want to build you an app for
work orders. It would have a dashboard, notifications, photo uploads, and a
map of the building so you can see every problem at once. It would be really
cool and I think it would save you a lot of time.

Can we meet?

Thanks,
Jordan
```

What is wrong, part by part: no subject line. No reason for choosing this person. It pitches four
features before anyone knows the problem. It says nothing about time or supervision. "Can we meet?"
gives no place and no way to decline.

**After:**

```
Subject: Request: a short conversation about building repair requests

Dear <title and last name>,

My name is Jordan, and I am a senior in the AI Automation and Software
Development program here at the career center. My instructor, <instructor
name>, is copied on this message.

For my senior capstone, I build a working piece of software for a real
person with a real problem. I am writing to you because I have seen repair
requests left on sticky notes on your office door, and I wondered whether
some of them get lost before they reach you.

Would you be willing to talk with me for about twenty minutes, here at
school, about how repair requests reach you today? If it turns out to be a
good fit, being my project's stakeholder would mean reviewing a written
proposal, about fifteen minutes of contact most weeks until the end of the
school year, one check near the end to confirm the work does what we
agreed, and a short training session. There is no cost to you.

It is completely fine to say no. Thank you for considering it.

Jordan <last name>
Senior, AI Automation and Software Development
<school name>
```

That draft is 189 words below the subject line, counting each placeholder as written. It is near
the limit, so it has no room for anything extra. Notice what it does not contain: no feature, no technical word, no promise.

## Worked example 2 · The apology spiral, rewritten

*Composite.* The candidate is a shift lead at the pizza shop where the student works. The prep list
lives on a whiteboard that gets wiped by accident.

**Before:**

```
Subject: sorry to bother you

Hi, sorry to bother you, I know you are super busy and this is probably a
weird ask, sorry. I have a school project and I was wondering if maybe you
could help me with it if you have time, sorry again, no worries if not!!
```

**After, the part that changed:**

```
Subject: Request: a senior project about the prep list

Dear <title and last name>,

My name is Maya. You know me from Tuesday and Thursday closing shifts. I am
writing from my school account because my senior capstone is supervised by
my instructor, <instructor name>, who is copied here.

I am writing to you because the prep list on the whiteboard has been wiped
by accident at least twice while I was on shift, and I wondered how often it
happens and what it costs the kitchen.
...
If your manager would need to approve this, I am glad to ask them the same
way.
```

**Two lessons.** Say sorry zero times in a request. A request is not an offense. And even when you
already know the person from work, the message still goes through school email. The rule protects
you and them, and a text at work is not a record.

## Worked example 3 · The reply to a yes, and the reply to a no

**The yes.** Reply the same day. Offer two specific times inside your class block, at school, with
your instructor present. Say what the conversation is for. Promise the summary.

```
Subject: Re: Request: a short conversation about building repair requests

Dear <title and last name>,

Thank you for saying yes. Could we meet on Friday during my class block,
between <start time> and <end time>, in <room>? My instructor will be there.
If Friday does not work, Monday at the same time works too.

I will ask how repair requests reach you today. I will not bring a finished
idea. Afterward I will send you a short summary so you can correct anything
I got wrong.

Thank you,
Jordan
```

Then add the reply to your contact log, tell your instructor, and tell your parent or guardian who
your stakeholder is. The fields to record are in the worksheet's
[When they say yes](../05-labs/MCCTC_145010_Template_StakeholderFinding.md#when-they-say-yes) section.

**The no.** One line. No argument, no "are you sure", no second pitch.

```
Subject: Re: Request: a short conversation about building repair requests

Dear <title and last name>,

Thank you for reading my message and for letting me know.

Jordan
```

Then write to your backup candidate **the same day**, after your instructor reads that message too.

**The maybe.** "Ask me again next week" is not a yes. Treat it as no reply. Tell your instructor at
Thursday's standup.

## The wrong version, and what it costs

The pitch in worked example 1 costs you in one of two ways.

- **They say no to the app.** "I do not need an app" ends the conversation before you learned the
  problem, which might have been real.
- **They say yes to the app.** Now you have promised a dashboard, notifications, photos, and a map
  before a scope check. On Tuesday of Week 8 your instructor tells you it will not finish, and you
  have to walk back a promise to an adult you have not met yet.

A draft that says "text me at my cell" costs more. It breaks the school-channel rule, and your
instructor stops the message before it goes out.

---

## Why the wrong version is tempting

The app is the exciting part. You have been imagining it since Monday, and describing it feels like
proof you are capable. Apologizing feels polite, because you are asking a favor. And a subject line
feels optional, because most of what you write is texts, which have none.

The fix is to write for the reader. The reader needs to know, in order, who, why them, what, how
long, and who supervises. Nothing else earns its place in the first message.

## Words the WebXam uses

| Exam word | What it means in this message |
|---|---|
| **Professional correspondence** | A message written for a work purpose, in a form the reader expects: subject, greeting, point, request, close |
| **Stakeholder** | A person affected by the project or with authority over it. Your first message is how you find one |
| **Solicit opinions** | Ask for someone's view. Your message asks about their problem, not your idea |
| **Network etiquette** | The conventions of electronic messages: a clear subject, a proper greeting, no slang, no all-caps |
| **Intended audience and purpose** | Who reads it and what it must get done. Here: a busy adult, and a yes or no to one conversation |
| **Networking** | Building professional relationships. A courteous first message is the start of one, even if the answer is no |

## Self-check

**1.** A classmate's draft opens with three sentences about the app they want to build. Which of the
five questions is the draft answering first, and why does that order cost a reply?

**2.** Your candidate is your manager at work, and you see them tomorrow. Why does the request still
go by school email?

**3.** A candidate replies, "Maybe. Ask me again next week." What do you record, and what do you do
before Friday?

### Answers

**1.** It answers "what are you asking for" with a product, before "who are you" and "why them". The
reader has to evaluate an app they never asked for, before they know who is writing. That invites a
quick no or a yes to a promise you cannot keep yet.

**2.** Every contact goes through school channels with your instructor copied. That protects you and
the stakeholder, and it creates a written record. A spoken request at work is not recorded, and a yes
is not a yes until it is in writing.

**3.** Record the reply in the contact log as a reply with no commitment. Treat it as no reply for the
Friday milestone. Tell your instructor at the next standup, and be ready to contact your backup
candidate once your instructor agrees.
