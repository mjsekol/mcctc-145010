# Clinic · Who Your Five Do Not Represent
## 145010 Senior Capstone · Clinic · Week 15, Wednesday

**The signal:** a recruiting table where all five participants are the same kind of person, such as
five students from one other program, or five adults who all use a computer every day at work.

**Slides:** This clinic has no slide outline. It runs from the board.

**If you missed it,** you can learn the skill from this file alone.

**Competencies:** 1.5.5 (recognize the ways bias and discrimination may influence productivity and
profitability), 1.5.6 (analyze work tasks for understanding from a different cultural perspective),
2.7.4 (how browsers and devices, including screen readers, affect a page), 6.5.10 (usability tests
checking accessibility, ease of use, and navigation)

**Every example below is a composite.** The Northside Community Garden and the Oak Hollow Animal
Rescue are invented organizations.

---

## The idea in plain language

**Your five participants can only tell you about people like them.** So you write down, plainly, who
they are not like. That list goes in your findings. It tells a reader where your results stop, and it
tells you what to be careful about before you claim anything.

## Why it exists

Five sessions find real problems. They do not find every problem, and the problems they miss are not
random. **They are the problems of the people you did not watch.**

If all five of your participants read English fluently, use a mouse, and have good eyesight, you
learn nothing about a volunteer who reads English as a second language, uses only a keyboard, or
zooms the page to 200 percent. Those people still use the garden sign-up. A product that works only
for the people its builder happened to test with has a bias built in, and nobody chose it on purpose.

That is what competency 1.5.5 means in a real project: **who a product serves well can depend on who
was in the room while it was built.**

**This is uncomfortable to write.** It feels like admitting your study was weak. It is the opposite. A
study that states its limits is one a reader can trust.

---

## How to find the gaps

Ask these about your five, one at a time. Use kinds, never details about a person.

```
Device       phone, tablet, laptop, lab machine, operator panel
Input        mouse, touch, keyboard only, screen reader, voice
Vision       zoom or large text, color perception, glare on a shop floor
Language     first language, reading comfort, the words their job uses
Experience   daily computer use, rarely online, new to this organization
Situation    in a hurry, interrupted, standing, wearing gloves, on slow Wi-Fi
Age range    only when it matters to the task, and only as a broad range
```

Then compare that with who your stakeholder says actually uses the project. **The gap between those
two lists is the statement you write.**

## Worked example 1 · five who are all alike

```
| P  | Kind of participant | Device | Input |
| P1 | other program       | laptop | mouse |
| P2 | other program       | laptop | mouse |
| P3 | other program       | laptop | mouse |
| P4 | building adult      | laptop | mouse |
| P5 | other program       | laptop | mouse |

The coordinator says: most volunteers sign up on phones, on Friday evening,
and several are retired.
```

**The result:** not one session used a phone, which is how most real users arrive. Nobody was a
retired volunteer. The honest statement:

```
Who these five do not represent: nobody used a phone, although the coordinator
says most volunteers do. Nobody was a retired volunteer. Nobody used a keyboard
only or a screen reader. Findings about layout and reading apply to laptop
users with a mouse; phone layout was not observed with a real person.
```

And the student's fix, since it is only Wednesday: move P5 to Thursday on a phone, and ask the
coordinator for one retired volunteer as the backup.

## Worked example 2 · a gap you cannot close this week

The Oak Hollow Animal Rescue adoption page. The stakeholder mentions that some families who adopt
speak Spanish at home. None of the five do, and the student cannot recruit a Spanish-speaking
participant through school channels by Thursday.

```
Who these five do not represent: nobody whose first language is not English.
The rescue coordinator says some adopting families speak Spanish at home.
This study cannot say whether the adoption form is clear to them.
Added to future improvements: FI-6, test the form with Spanish-speaking
families, through the rescue, after handoff.
```

**The result:** no invented finding, no claim the study cannot support, and a named next step the
stakeholder can act on. That is 1.5.6 in practice: you noticed that the task might read differently
from another perspective, and you said what it would take to find out.

## Worked example 3 · claims, before and after

```
TOO BIG       "Users found the sign-up clear."
HONEST        "4 of 5 people I watched, all using laptops, signed up in under a
               minute with no wrong attempts."

TOO BIG       "The site is accessible."
HONEST        "web-check reports 0 violations. 1 of 5 participants tried a task
               keyboard only and lost focus after Claim (P3 L7). No participant
               used a screen reader."

TOO BIG       "80 percent of users completed every task."
HONEST        "4 of 5 participants completed every task."
```

**Counts out of five, never percentages.** "4 of 5 people I watched" is exactly what you know.
"80 percent of users" sounds like a survey you did not do.

## The wrong version, and what it produces

```markdown
## Limits
Our five participants were a good mix, so the results should apply to everyone.
```

**What it produces:** a claim nobody can check and the table contradicts. At Milestone Review 4 or
in the defense, one question ends it: "How many of your five used a phone?" If the answer is zero,
and the stakeholder says most users do, the findings about layout are about the wrong people. The
rubric's honest questions reward a student who saw this coming. They do not reward "a good mix."

## Why the wrong version is tempting

You worked hard to find five people. Admitting a gap feels like undoing that work. It is also
tempting to think a mix of ages or programs covers everything, when the gap that matters is a device
or an input method. **Naming the gap costs one paragraph. Being asked about it without having named
it costs the question.**

## What to do in your project today

1. Put your five in a table like example 1: kind, device, input. No names.
2. Ask your stakeholder, if you have not, how real users arrive: device, time of day, situation.
3. Compare the two lists. Write "Who these five do not represent" in your protocol and findings.
4. If a gap can still be closed this week, close it with your Thursday sessions or your backup.
5. If it cannot, add a future improvement that says how it could be.
6. Check your findings draft for any percentage. Replace it with a count out of five.
7. See part 1 of the [Usability Test Protocol](../05-labs/MCCTC_145010_Template_UsabilityTestProtocol.md)
   and the limits step at the end of part 7.

## Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Representation** | Which kinds of people your participants include |
| **Gap** | A kind of real user none of your participants were like |
| **Bias in a product** | A product that serves some people worse because of how it was made, often by accident |
| **Limit statement** | The written list of who your study does not speak for |
| **Count out of five** | The honest way to report a result from five sessions |
