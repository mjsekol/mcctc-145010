# Clinic · Presenting the Proposal
## 145010 Senior Capstone · Week 8, Thursday · 15 minutes · Define

**Slides for this clinic:** This clinic has no slide outline. It runs at the board.

meeting booked and no plan for it.

**If you missed it,** you can learn the skill from this file alone.

complete forms and reports).

---

## Why this exists

**Your stakeholder has the proposal. They may not have read it.** Most busy adults skim a long
document. The presentation is where the two of you agree, out loud, on what you will build, what you
will not, and how both of you will know it worked. Tomorrow they sign. What they sign should be what
they understood today.

A presentation also exposes the criterion two people read differently, and the "can it also..."
request that would quietly double your scope. Both are cheap to handle today and expensive in
Week 16.

**Presenting is not reading the document aloud.** Twenty minutes of you reading section by section
loses the room by section 3. You have about ten minutes, then their questions.

---

## The skill in plain language

**The ten-minute plan.** Six parts, in this order.

| Minutes | Part | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Open** | Thank them. Say the purpose: "to check that this proposal is what you need before you sign." Say how long: ten minutes, then questions |
| 2 | **Their problem** | Say the problem in their words, from your meeting record. Ask: "Did I get that right?" |
| 2 | **What you get, and what you do not** | In scope, out of scope, and the stretch list. Features with their benefit to them |
| 3 | **The criteria, aloud** | Read each acceptance criterion. Ask them to say what it means to them. Rewrite any word you read differently |
| 1 | **What I need from you** | Their part: reply times, the Week 10 review, the Week 16 acceptance run, the Week 17 training |
| 1 | **Cost and support** | No cost to them. What you will fix after handoff, and until when. Nothing more is promised |

**Handling "can it also...?"** It will come. Never say yes in the moment. Use four moves:

1. **Write it down** where they can see you do it.
2. **Say what it would cost:** "Adding that would put the low list at risk of not being tested
   properly."
3. **Offer the stretch list:** "I can put it on the stretch list, which I start only when everything
   we agree today passes."
4. **Offer a trade if they insist:** "If it matters more than something already in scope, we can
   swap. I would check the hours with my instructor before I change the proposal."

A swap changes the proposal before it is signed. The version number goes up, the decision log says
why, and your instructor confirms it still finishes before the new version goes out.

**Where it happens.** In person at school with your instructor present, on a call your instructor
approved, or, if neither is possible, in writing with specific questions. A proposal emailed with no
reply was not presented.

**After, the same day.** Write the meeting record. Fill in section 20 of the proposal. Send a short
summary. Log it.

## Worked example 1 · The plan on one card

*Composite, not a real organization or person.* The Parts Bin Board for a volunteer-run bike repair
co-op. The student brings this card, not a script.

```
OPEN      Thank you. Check the proposal is what you need before you
          sign. Ten minutes, then your questions.
PROBLEM   "Nobody tells me." Repairs stall when a bin is empty.
          Some volunteers use the desktop by the door. Did I get that right?
GET       Mark a bin low from the shop link, no name asked.
          Your low list, with a restock button, behind a sign-in.
          Benefit: you see every empty bin without walking the shelves.
NOT       No volunteer accounts. No ordering. No customer information.
STRETCH   Photo of the bin. Barcode scanning. Only after everything passes.
CRITERIA  Read AC-1 to AC-8 aloud. "What does this one mean to you?"
NEED      Replies within two school days. Requirements review Week 10.
          Acceptance run Week 16. Training Week 17.
COST      Nothing to the co-op. I fix reported problems until the end
          of Week 18. Nothing after that unless agreed through school.
CLOSE     Summary today. Could you sign by tomorrow?
```

The card fits in ten minutes because every line is a prompt, not a paragraph.

## Worked example 2 · "Can it also send me a text?"

*Composite.* Halfway through, the coordinator asks whether the board could text them when a bin is
marked low.

**Before, what a student says without a plan:**

```
Yeah, totally, I can add that. It shouldn't be too hard.
```

That sentence adds an unknown task, an outside messaging account, a credential, and the
coordinator's phone number to a project whose scope check finished two days ago. The coordinator now
expects texts, and nothing in writing says otherwise.

**After:**

```
That is a good idea, and I have written it down. Texts would need an
outside messaging service and your phone number stored in the system,
and adding them would put the low list at risk of not being tested
properly before Week 16.

I can add it to the list of future improvements that I hand over at the
end. The low list will show every empty bin whenever you open it. Would
that work for now?
```

The coordinator agreed. The student added "text alerts" to the proposal's out-of-scope list with the
reason, and recorded it in the decision log that afternoon.

## Worked example 3 · The same-day record, and section 20

The record follows the [Meeting Record template](../05-labs/MCCTC_145010_Template_MeetingRecord.md).
Its key sections:

```markdown
## What they said
- "Yes, that's the problem."
- Asked whether the board could text them. Agreed to a future improvements entry.
- Read AC-7 aloud and asked, "Does 'the desktop by the door' mean the old one or the new one?"

## Decisions
| # | Decision | How it was reached |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Text alerts are out of scope and listed as a future improvement | I proposed and they accepted |
| 2 | AC-7 names the donated desktop by the front door, not the office laptop | They decided |

## Actions
| Action | Who | By when |
|---|---|---|
| Send proposal version 1.1 and agreement version 1.1 | Me | Week 8, Thursday |
| Sign the agreement | Shop coordinator | Week 8, Friday |
```

And section 20 of the proposal, filled in:

```markdown
## 20. Presented and approved
- **Presented:** Week 8, Thursday, in person at school, instructor present
- **Questions the stakeholder asked:** Could it send texts? Which desktop does AC-7 mean?
- **What changed because of the presentation:** Text alerts added to out of scope.
  AC-7 wording made exact. Version 1.1.
- **Stakeholder response:** approved with changes
- **Acceptance agreement version this proposal supports:** 1.1
```

The AC-7 change clarifies a word and adds no work, so it did not need a new scope check. The student
still told the instructor before sending version 1.1.

## The wrong version, and what it costs

The student emails the proposal Wednesday, hears nothing, and writes "presented" in section 20 on
Thursday. On Friday the coordinator signs without reading it closely. In Week 16 the coordinator
asks where the texts are, because they mentioned texts to a volunteer and assumed it was included.
Nobody ever said out loud that it was not.

Your instructor watches for exactly this signal: a proposal "presented" by email with no reply.
Presenting in writing is allowed when a meeting is impossible, but it means specific questions and
a written answer, not silence.

---

## Why the wrong version is tempting

The proposal is long and you have already sent it. Presenting it feels like repeating yourself.
Meeting an adult to be questioned on your plan is uncomfortable. And saying yes to a request feels
like good customer service.

The presentation is not a repeat. It is the only time you hear how they read what you wrote. And
the best customer service is a promise you keep.

## Words the WebXam uses

| Exam word | What it means in this skill |
|---|---|
| **Comprehensive proposal** | A proposal that covers scope, audience, needs, outcomes, budget, timeline, and responsibilities |
| **Present to stakeholders** | Walk the people with authority through the proposal and get their response |
| **Formal presentation** | A planned presentation with a purpose, a structure, and a time limit |
| **Features and benefits** | What it does, and what that means for the stakeholder |
| **Warranty** | What you promise to fix, and until when. Here, stated in plain words |
| **Consensus** | Agreement on next steps, recorded as decisions |

## Self-check

**1.** Your stakeholder asks, "Could it also track which volunteer took the last tube?" Write what
you say.

**2.** Why do you ask the stakeholder to read the criteria aloud instead of reading them yourself
and asking "Is that okay?"

**3.** After the presentation, the only change was a clearer word in one criterion. Does the
version number change? Why?

### Answers

**1.** Something like: "I have written that down. Tracking who took it would mean storing volunteers'
names, and it would put the low list at risk of not being finished. I can list it as a future
improvement. The low list will still show every empty bin." Never yes in the moment.

**2.** "Is that okay?" invites a polite yes. Reading aloud and saying what it means shows how they
understood it, which is how you catch a word two people read differently.

**3.** Yes. Any change after the stakeholder has seen the document raises the version, and the
decision log says why. The version they sign must be the version they discussed.
