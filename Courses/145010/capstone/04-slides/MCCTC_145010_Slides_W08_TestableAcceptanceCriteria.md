# Acceptance Criteria That Can Fail

## Slide 1: Will this pass in Week 16?
- Your draft says: the board works well
- Your stakeholder signs it today
- In Week 16, you both try to check it
- Nobody can say whether it passed
Speaker notes: Look at your own draft agreement right now. Find the criterion you are least sure about. In Week 16 you and your stakeholder sit down and run every one of these, and each gets recorded as passed, not passed, or not run. Then your stakeholder decides whether to accept the project, and ten points of your grade come from that record. A criterion that says the board works well cannot be run. It can only be argued about. Today is the last day to fix that, because the agreement goes out this afternoon.

## Slide 2: Four criteria nobody can run
```
The board works well.
The board is secure.
The board is user-friendly.
The list loads fast.
```
Speaker notes: These four show up in almost every first draft. Every one of them is something you believe. None of them tells a stranger what to do, and none of them could ever come out not passed, because there is no result that proves them wrong. Works well compared to what? Secure against whom? User-friendly for which user? Fast by whose watch? Keep these four in your head. We are going to turn each one into something that can fail.

## Slide 3: The shape
```
Given  a starting situation
When   someone does something
Then   an exact result anyone can check

Given  the shop link open on the desktop by the door
When   a volunteer selects bin B-04 and chooses Mark low
Then   the page says "B-04 marked low"
```
Speaker notes: This is the shape from the acceptance agreement template. Given sets the scene. When is one action. Then is the result, written so exactly that a stranger could look at the screen and say yes or no. The example is from a composite project, a parts board for a volunteer bike repair co-op. Notice there is no code in it, no table name, no framework word. Your stakeholder has to understand every word, because they are the one who runs it with you.

## Slide 4: Two tests for every criterion
- Could a stranger run it from the words alone?
- Could it come out not passed?
- Two no answers mean rewrite it
- One no answer also means rewrite it
Speaker notes: Here is the whole skill in two questions. First, could somebody who has never met you run this criterion without asking you anything? Second, is there any possible result that would make it fail? If a criterion cannot fail, it is not a test. It is a compliment you are paying yourself. Go through your draft with these two questions before you send it. Any criterion that gets a no on either one gets rewritten today.

## Slide 5: What the wrong version costs
```
WEEK 16 ACCEPTANCE RUN (composite)

AC-1  The board works well.        NOT RUN   no way to check it
AC-4  The board is user-friendly.  NOT RUN   stakeholder: "some volunteers
                                              found it confusing"
                                              student: "it is user-friendly"

Stakeholder decision: not accepted until AC-1 and AC-4 are rewritten
```
Speaker notes: This is a composite, built from the way this goes, not a real student. Look at what happened. Nobody was lying. The stakeholder honestly thought it was confusing, and the student honestly thought it was fine. With nothing to run, there is nothing to settle it. And the agreement says a criterion is never changed during the run. Any change has to be agreed in writing first. So this project reaches its acceptance week with two criteria nobody can run, and the acceptance score is built from exactly this record.

## Slide 6: Numbers instead of adjectives
- Fast becomes within 3 seconds, timed
- User-friendly becomes a first-time volunteer finishes in 2 minutes
- Accessible becomes every step works by keyboard alone
- Secure becomes signed-out visitors see no bin information
Speaker notes: Every adjective hides a number or an observable result. Find it. Fast becomes a time, with how it is timed. User-friendly becomes a person who has never seen it, doing a task with no help, inside a time limit. Accessible becomes a way of using it, like keyboard only. Secure becomes a specific person who cannot see a specific thing. Once there is a number or an observable result, the criterion can fail, which means it can also pass without an argument.

## Slide 7: The four you must have
- Main task: the thing they named, working
- A failure: what happens when something breaks
- Privacy or security: what is never collected or shown
- Devices: exactly which ones, used exactly how
Speaker notes: Six to ten criteria is usual, and at least one has to be each of these four kinds. Nobody forgets the main task. The other three are the ones that get skipped. The failure criterion matters most, because it shows whether your system tells the truth when something goes wrong. The devices criterion names the actual machines. The co-op composite names the desktop by the door with its browser and width, and a phone at 360 pixels wide.

## Slide 8: A failure criterion
```
AC-3
Given  the board is in its test mode with the database switched off
When   a volunteer chooses Mark low
Then   the page says the report was not saved and to tell the coordinator.
       It never says the report was saved.
```
Speaker notes: This is the one students skip. Read the Then line twice. The worst thing a system can do when it fails is claim it succeeded, because the volunteer walks away believing the coordinator knows. So this criterion checks honesty. Notice the Given. You build a test mode so your stakeholder can watch the failure safely, in the room, without anyone unplugging anything. That test mode is part of your project, and it goes in your task list.

## Slide 9: Read it aloud together
- Same words can mean two things
- Notified: you meant the list, they heard text
- Ask them to read each one aloud
- Rewrite any word two people read differently
Speaker notes: Here is a composite. A draft said the coordinator is notified when a bin is marked low. The student meant the bin shows up on the list. The coordinator read it aloud and said, good, so I get a text. Same sentence, two promises. Tomorrow when you present, ask your stakeholder to read every criterion aloud and say what it means to them. Any word that two people read differently gets rewritten before anyone signs.
