## Slide 1: Who is waiting for what you build next?
- For two years, your audience was a teacher
- Starting today, it is a person with a problem
- They did not assign it. They need it
- In twelve weeks, you hand it over
Speaker notes: Think about every project you have built in this program. Somebody assigned it, somebody knew the answer, and somebody was going to read it whether it was good or not. That was me. Starting today, none of that is true. You are going to find a real person with a real problem, agree in writing what finished means, build it, and hand it to them. In Week 18 you stand in front of a panel and answer questions about every part of it. This is the thing you will show an employer or a college. So let us talk about how it works and how it goes wrong.

## Slide 2: The requirement, whatever you build
- A real problem for a real person outside this room
- Deployed and reachable by someone who is not you
- Acceptance criteria signed by that person in Week 8
- Survives thirty days without you touching it
- Presented and defended, every part of it
Speaker notes: Five lines, and every capstone meets all five. There are two more in your specification: full documentation, and the whole DMAIC cycle with evidence every week. Notice what is not on this list. Nothing here says impressive. Nothing says large. A small project that a real person accepted, that ran for thirty days on its own, and that you can explain line by line is an excellent capstone. And one rule sits above all of it, the same rule as every course in this program. The only way to fail outright is to submit work you cannot explain.

## Slide 3: The twelve weeks
```
Weeks 7-8    DEFINE           stakeholder, proposal, SIGNED AGREEMENT
Weeks 9-10   MEASURE/ANALYZE  baseline, requirements, design, test plan
Weeks 11-14  IMPROVE          four sprints, weekly updates, two reviews
                              SCOPE LOCKED end of Week 12
Weeks 15-16  CONTROL          five real users, corrections, acceptance
                              WebXam post-test Week 16
Weeks 17-18  CONTROL          rollout, training, handoff, presentation
```
Speaker notes: This is DMAIC, the same framework you have used since junior year, stretched over twelve weeks. Look at how little of it is building. Four weeks. That is about fifty hours. The two weeks before it decide whether those fifty hours build the right thing, and the four weeks after it decide whether anybody can use what you built. Three dates on here do not move. A signed agreement by the end of Week 8. Scope locked at the end of Week 12. And the WebXam post-test in Week 16, which is also how your college credit gets triggered.

Speaker notes: Read the left column and then read it again. Seventy percent of this grade is not code. A working project with no stakeholder, no documentation, and no defense is a failing capstone. I want you to hear that today and not in Week 17. Every template in the labs folder exists because it is evidence for one of these four lines. The full scoring guide is public. ## Slide 6: The first way it fails: scope
```
PROPOSAL, WEEK 8 (composite)
  Six sensors, trend charts, predictive failure warnings,
  text alerts, logins for every employee, a mobile app,
  and the panel starts the cooling fan automatically.

SPRINT REVIEW, WEEK 14
  Sensors wired: 2 of 6     Panel screens: 3 of 9
  Predictive model: not started     Deployed: no
  Acceptance criteria passing: 1 of 11
```
Speaker notes: This is a composite, built from the way this usually goes, not a real student. Every line of that proposal sounds reasonable on its own. That is what makes it dangerous. Now look at Week 14. Nothing crashed. Nobody slacked off. The student worked hard for six weeks and is at about forty percent with no time left, and one of those ideas, starting a fan automatically, was never allowed in the first place. Scope gets killed in Week 8, in a conference with me, where I will tell you first whether you will finish. You will not enjoy it. It is the most useful fifteen minutes of the capstone.

## Slide 8: The second way it fails: nobody real
- A named, contactable person by Friday of Week 7
- Approved by me before you write to them
- If "students" or "small businesses" is your user, stop
- No reply by Thursday? Tell me in standup
Speaker notes: A capstone with an imaginary user drifts. Every decision gets made by guessing, and every guess is the one that is most fun to build. So by Friday of this week, you have a real person who has replied in writing. The earliest warning sign is a student who describes the user as a category. Students. Small businesses. Gamers. If that is you, go back to the worksheet today and pick someone you can actually reach. And if nobody has written back by Thursday, say so in standup. I keep a list of adults who have agreed to be stakeholders. Nobody leaves this week without one.

## Slide 9: Rules that are not negotiable
- School email only, with me copied
- Never alone with a stakeholder
- Your parent or guardian knows who they are
- No personal data in any AI tool
- Models run locally. No commercial AI accounts
Speaker notes: You are about to work with adults who are not in this building every day. These rules protect you, and they protect them. Every message goes through your school account with me copied, including the first one, which I read before you send it. Meetings happen at school, on a call I approved, or on a visit the school arranged. Never alone. Your family knows who your stakeholder is. And the two rules you have heard since junior year still hold. No personal information goes into any AI tool, and any model your project uses runs on our hardware, because the commercial developer services require users to be eighteen.
