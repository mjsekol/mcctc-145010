## Slide 1: Tomorrow a stranger uses your project
- You know where every button is
- They do not, and you cannot unknow it
- In twenty seconds you will want to help
- Helping erases the thing you came to measure
Speaker notes: Tomorrow, somebody who has never seen your project sits down in front of it. You built every screen, so you know exactly where Saturday is and what Claim means. They do not. Here is what will happen inside you. About twenty seconds after they get stuck, you will want to point. Today is about not pointing, because the moment you help, you have lost the one thing you needed to know: whether they would have found it without you.

## Slide 2: The rules are conditions, not advice
- A spoken yes after the consent script
- Nothing recorded. Paper only
- Participants are P1 to P5. No names
- No classmates in the formal five
- Nothing from a session goes into AI
Speaker notes: These rules stop a session if you break one. The person says yes out loud after you read the consent script exactly as written. No video, no audio, no screen capture, no photos. You write on paper. Participants are P1 through P5, and nothing identifies them. Classmates are for today's pilot, not the five. Nothing from a session goes into any AI tool, not the notes and not a summary. And two more from the protocol: you are never alone with an adult you do not know, and you thank every participant in writing.

## Slide 3: The consent script, word for word
```
I am testing a project I built, and I am not testing you. Anything
that goes wrong is information I need. I am going to ask you to do a
few things and then stay quiet while you do them, and that is on
purpose, not rude. I am not recording anything and I am not writing
down your name. You can stop at any point, for any reason, and you do
not have to tell me why. Is that okay?
```
Speaker notes: Read this exactly as written. When people paraphrase, the sentences they drop are the recording one and the stopping one, and those are the two that matter most. Then wait for a yes out loud. A nod is not a yes. If they hesitate, the answer is no, and you thank them and stop.

## Slide 4: Write goals, not instructions
```
INSTRUCTION (do not use)   Click the Saturday tab and press Claim on bed 4.
GOAL (use)                 You can help at the garden this Saturday morning.
                           Sign yourself up for a bed that still needs someone.
Completed when             one open Saturday bed shows their first name
Expected time              under 60 seconds
```
Speaker notes: An instruction tests whether someone can follow directions. A goal tests whether your page shows them the way. Notice the goal never names a control. Write what completed looks like and how long you expect it to take, so you have something to compare against. Today's pilot with a classmate is where you find the task that gives the answer away, or the one your project cannot actually do.

## Slide 5: What you may say
- Nothing, which is usually right
- What are you trying to do right now?
- What did you expect to happen?
- Take your time
Speaker notes: This is the whole list. Silence is the default and it is usually right. If you need to know what they are thinking, ask what they are trying to do right now, or what they expected to happen. Take your time is allowed. If they ask whether they are doing it right, say there is no right way and you want to see what they would actually do. If they are stuck for three minutes, say let us move to the next one, and write down where they were stuck.

## Slide 6: The wrong way, and what it cost
```
0:31  P2: "Where are the Saturday ones?"
      FACILITATOR: "Oh, you can use the tabs at the top. I put them
      there because the coordinator wanted days first. See, Saturday.
      Then press Claim."
0:42  success

Sheet says: Task 1 completed, 0:42, no problems.   <- false
```
Speaker notes: This is a composite, and it is the most common failure I see. The participant asks one question and the facilitator names a control, says you can, defends the design, and gives the answer. The sheet now says the task took forty-two seconds with no problems. That is false. The finding, that people do not see the day tabs, is gone. And the explanation, the reason the tabs are there, is not data. What they did is the data. Your reasons are not.

## Slide 7: The same moment, done right
```
0:31  P2: "Where are the Saturday ones?"
      FACILITATOR: "What are you trying to do right now?"
0:35  P2: "Find Saturday."            (facilitator says nothing)
0:52  hesitates 12 s over the week arrows
1:04  goes to next week, comes back   (wrong attempt 2)
1:26  "Bed 4 is yours"
1:30  P2: "Oh, that was fine."

Task 1: yes · 1:26 · 2 wrong attempts · 2 hesitations
```
Speaker notes: Same participant, same question. One allowed question, then silence. Now look at what you learn. Eighty-six seconds against an expected sixty, two wrong attempts, and two hesitations. And at the end the participant says it was fine. Both are true. The left column, what they did, is what you count. The right column, what they said, is recorded word for word and labelled as an opinion. When they disagree, what they did wins.

## Slide 8: When you slip, and you might
- Say: Sorry, ignore me. Please carry on
- Tick the rule-break box on the sheet
- Note which task it happened on
- That task's finding carries the caveat
- Stop at fifteen minutes, even mid-task
Speaker notes: You might slip. It happens to experienced researchers. If you catch yourself explaining, say sorry, ignore me, please carry on the way you were. Then be honest on the sheet. Tick the rule-break box and write which task. That session is still usable. The finding from that task carries a caveat, and your findings are more trustworthy because you said so. And stop at fifteen minutes, even in the middle of a task, because stopping on time is how the next person says yes.

## Slide 9: What you are about to build
- Finish the protocol: four to six goal tasks
- One task on keyboard, phone, or deep navigation
- Pilot with a classmate and fix what breaks
- Confirm five sessions and a backup, with times
- Print sheets, practice the script aloud, commit
Speaker notes: Here is your build period. Finish your protocol with four to six goal tasks, each with a starting state, what completed looks like, an expected time, and the requirement it tests. At least one task covers accessibility or navigation. Pilot it with a classmate, label it a pilot, and fix every task the pilot broke. Confirm five real sessions and a backup for tomorrow through Thursday. Print one observation sheet per person, read the consent script out loud twice, and commit.
